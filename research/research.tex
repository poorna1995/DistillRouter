\title{DistillRouter: Efficient LLM Routing via Knowledge Distillation}

\author{}

\begin{document}
\maketitle

\begin{abstract}
LLM routing improves the cost-performance tradeoff of large-scale LLM serving by directing queries to the least expensive capable model. However, routing itself incurs inference overhead, and increasingly sophisticated routers can become deployment bottlenecks. We propose DistillRouter, a routing-policy distillation framework that transfers routing policy from an expensive teacher router to a lightweight student router. DistillRouter converts routing into a supervised classification problem, enabling deployment through a single forward pass while preserving routing quality. We evaluate DistillRouter on GSM8K and MATH under both binary and three-way routing settings using multiple student model scales. Across all configurations, distilled routers achieve routing accuracy comparable to or better than the teacher while reducing routing latency by up to 150×. We further investigate reasoning-augmented supervision and find that although reasoning traces improve policy transfer at larger model scales, they do not consistently improve routing correctness. Our results demonstrate that routing policies can be effectively compressed into compact models, providing an efficient and practical solution for large-scale LLM serving.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Large Language Models (LLMs) have become a foundational component of modern AI systems, enabling applications such as conversational assistants, customer-support agents, coding assistants, search systems, and enterprise copilots. Their ability to perform a wide range of language understanding and generation tasks has led to rapid adoption across both consumer and enterprise settings. However, deploying LLMs at scale remains challenging because inference cost and latency grow substantially with model size. In practice, many production systems must process large numbers of user queries while operating under strict cost and responsiveness constraints.
A fundamental characteristic of LLM workloads is that query difficulty is highly heterogeneous. While some requests require complex reasoning and benefit from powerful frontier models, many others can be answered correctly by smaller and less expensive models. Consequently, routing every query to the most capable model often wastes computational resources and increases serving costs. This observation has motivated the development of LLM routing, where a router dynamically selects the model expected to provide the best cost-performance trade-off for a given query. By sending simple queries to inexpensive models and reserving larger models for more challenging requests, routing systems can substantially reduce serving costs while maintaining response quality. Recent work has demonstrated the effectiveness of routing in improving deployment efficiency across diverse LLM systems.
Despite these benefits, routing introduces a new deployment challenge. Determining which model should answer a query is itself an inference problem. Existing routing approaches learn routing policies from preference data, reward signals, or reinforcement learning and often rely on additional models to predict routing decisions. For example, RouteLLM learns routing policies from preference data, while Router-R1 instantiates the router itself as a capable LLM that performs reasoning and routing actions during inference. Although such approaches improve routing accuracy, they also introduce additional computational overhead that is incurred for every incoming query. In large-scale deployments, the router therefore becomes part of the serving pipeline and can reduce some of the efficiency gains that routing is intended to provide.

This observation raises an important question:
\begin{quote}
    \textbf{\textit{Can routing policy learned from expensive model be compressed into a lightweight model.}}

\end{quote}

Knowledge distillation offers a natural solution. Rather than deploying a computationally expensive router online, an accurate teacher router can be used offline to generate supervision for a smaller student router. The student router learns the routing policy during training and subsequently performs routing through a single forward pass at inference time. If successful, this shifts computational effort from deployment to training while retaining the benefits of a high-quality routing policy.

In this paper, we propose DistillRouter, a framework for building efficient LLM routers through knowledge distillation. We formulate routing as a routing-policy learning problem and transfer a routing policy from an expensive teacher router to a compact student router. We investigate two distillation strategies. The first, route-only distillation, transfers the teacher's routing decisions directly. The second, reasoning-augmented distillation, augments routing supervision with teacher-generated reasoning traces as an auxiliary training signal while maintaining identical deployment cost. This formulation enables a controlled study of whether richer supervision improves routing-policy distillation.

We evaluate DistillRouter across two benchmarks. Our results show that routing policies can be effectively compressed through distillation, producing student routers that substantially reduce inference latency while maintaining strong routing accuracy. We further study the role of reasoning supervision and analyze routing behavior using both policy-transfer and oracle-label evaluations.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{DistillRouter:} We propose a knowledge-distillation framework for compressing expensive LLM routing policies into lightweight deployable routers.
    \item \textbf{Efficient Routing via Distillation:} We demonstrate that routing policies can be effectively transferred to compact student routers, significantly reducing routing overhead while preserving strong routing accuracy.
    \item \textbf{Understanding Reasoning Supervision:} We provide an empirical study of reasoning-augmented routing-policy distillation and show that improved policy transfer does not necessarily imply improved routing accuracy.
\end{itemize}


\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related-work}

\paragraph{LLM Routing.} LLM routing aims to reduce inference cost by selecting the most appropriate model for each query. Recent work has shown that routing can significantly improve cost-quality tradeoffs compared to always using the most capable model. RouteLLM\citep{ong2024routellm} learns lightweight routers from human preference data and dynamically selects between stronger and weaker models at inference time. ZOOTER\citep{lu2024zooter}uses reward-model signals to train routing functions that dispatch queries to expert models. More recently, Router-R1\citep{zhang2025routerr1} formulates routing as a sequential decision-making problem and trains an LLM router through reinforcement learning, interleaving reasoning and routing actions.
While these approaches focus on improving routing decisions, they still require deploying a router at inference time. In contrast, we study whether an expensive routing policy can be compressed into a lightweight deployable router through knowledge distillation.

\paragraph{Routing Distillation and Reasoning Supervision.}

Knowledge distillation is widely used to transfer capabilities from large models to smaller ones. In LLM routing, however, prior work primarily learns routing functions from preference labels, reward signals, or reinforcement-learning objectives rather than explicitly distilling a routing policy from a larger teacher router.
Reasoning has also been explored as a mechanism for improving LLM decision making. In routing systems such as Router-R1, reasoning is generated during inference and contributes directly to routing decisions. By contrast, we investigate reasoning as a training-time-only auxiliary supervisory signal that is discarded at deployment. This allows us to study whether reasoning improves routing-policy transfer without increasing inference cost.

\section{Problem Formulation}
\label{sec:problem_formulation}

Consider a routing environment consisting of a set of candidate language models
\[
  \mathcal{M} = \{m_1, m_2, m_3\},
\]
ordered by increasing parameter count, capability, and serving cost. Given an incoming user query $x$, the goal of the routing system is to predict an appropriate routing label
\[
  y \in \mathcal{Y},
\]
that determines which model category should process the query. The routing objective is to assign each query to the least expensive model category capable of producing a correct answer. Routing labels therefore reflect the capability level required to answer a query correctly: queries that can be handled by lower-cost models should be assigned lower-cost routing labels, while more challenging queries should be assigned higher-capability routing labels.

An expensive \emph{teacher router} defines a routing policy
\[
  \pi_T(y \mid x),
\]
which maps queries to routing decisions. The teacher may employ larger language models, multi-stage prompting, or explicit reasoning to determine the appropriate routing label. Our objective is to learn a lightweight \emph{student router}
\[
  \pi_\theta(y \mid x),
\]
that approximates the teacher's routing policy while operating at substantially lower inference cost. Formally, the problem addressed in this work is:
\begin{quote}
  \emph{Given an expensive routing policy $\pi_T$, can we learn a compact routing policy $\pi_\theta$ that preserves routing accuracy while significantly reducing routing latency and computational overhead?}
\end{quote}

\textsc{DistillRouter} is independent of the specific routing label space. In this work, we evaluate two routing settings.

\subsection{Binary Routing}

Our primary configuration uses a binary label space
\[
  \mathcal{Y}_{\text{binary}} = \{\texttt{small}, \texttt{large}\}.
\]
Here, the \texttt{small} label represents queries whose cheapest correct assignment belongs to either the $m_1$ or $m_2$ category, while \texttt{large} represents queries that require the $m_3$ category. This formulation focuses on the key routing distinction between queries that can be handled by lower-cost models and those requiring the highest-capability model.

\subsection{Three-Way Routing}

To evaluate finer-grained routing decisions, we additionally consider
\[
  \mathcal{Y}_{\text{3way}} = \{\texttt{small}, \texttt{medium}, \texttt{large}\}.
\]
In this setting, the router directly predicts the model category assigned to a query, enabling more precise model selection across the candidate pool. The binary and three-way settings evaluate different levels of routing granularity: binary routing measures whether the router can distinguish between lower-cost and highest-capability model categories, whereas three-way routing evaluates whether the router can accurately differentiate among all available model categories.

\subsection{Oracle Labels}
\label{sec:oracle-routing-policy}

For each query, the oracle label is defined as the smallest model tier that produces the correct answer under our evaluation protocol. Specifically, given the candidate model set $\{m_1, m_2, m_3\}$, we execute all candidate models and assign the query to the least expensive model whose output matches the reference answer. If only the largest model answers correctly, the oracle label is \texttt{large}; if both $m_1$ and $m_2$ answer correctly, the oracle label is \texttt{small}. These oracle labels represent the optimal cost-aware routing decision for each query. Oracle labels are used exclusively for evaluation and provide an independent measure of routing accuracy — they are never used for training.

\section{Methodology}
\label{sec:methodology}

\subsection{DistillRouter Framework}
\label{sec:distillrouter_framework}

The goal of DistillRouter is to reduce the deployment overhead of LLM routing by transferring a routing policy from an expensive teacher router to a lightweight student router. Given a user query $x$, the teacher router estimates the minimum model category required to answer the query and generates supervisory signals for training. The student router then learns this routing policy and replaces the teacher at deployment time. DistillRouter consists of two stages: \textbf{teacher-policy generation}, in which an expensive teacher router processes training queries and generates routing supervision, optionally including teacher reasoning traces that explain its routing decision; and \textbf{routing-policy distillation}, in which a student router is trained to predict the teacher's routing labels. After training, only the student router is deployed, enabling routing through a single forward pass. The overall objective is to preserve routing accuracy while significantly reducing routing latency and computational cost.

\subsection{Routing-Policy Distillation}
\label{sec:distillation}

Let $x \in \mathcal{X}$ denote a query and let $\mathcal{Y} = \{\texttt{small}, \texttt{medium}, \texttt{large}\}$ denote the routing label space. Given a teacher routing policy $\pi_T(y \mid x)$, DistillRouter learns a student routing policy $\pi_\theta(y \mid x)$ that approximates it, $\pi_\theta(y \mid x) \approx \pi_T(y \mid x)$. The student router is trained using teacher-generated supervision and subsequently performs routing without requiring the teacher router at inference time.

\subsection{Teacher Router}
\label{sec:teacher-routing}
\label{sec:teacher_routing_policy}

The teacher router serves as the source of routing supervision. For each query $x$, the teacher performs two sequential steps. First, \emph{reasoning generation}: the teacher analyzes the query and generates a reasoning trace $r = f_{\text{reason}}(x)$ summarizing the characteristics of the query. These reasoning traces are used only as training supervision and are never used during deployment. Second, \emph{routing prediction}: conditioned on the query, the generated reasoning trace, and a set of calibration demonstrations $D_{cal}$, the teacher predicts a routing label $y = f_{\text{route}}(x, r, D_{cal})$ corresponding to the model category predicted to be sufficient for answering the query. Applying the teacher router across the training corpus produces a routing dataset $D_T = \{(x_i, y_i, r_i)\}_{i=1}^{N}$, where $x_i$ is the query, $y_i$ is the routing label, and $r_i$ is the teacher reasoning trace. This dataset provides the supervision used for routing-policy distillation.

\subsection{Student Router}
\label{sec:student-router}

The student router is initialized from a pretrained language-model backbone. Given a query $x = (t_1, \ldots, t_n)$, the transformer backbone produces contextual hidden representations $H = (h_1, \ldots, h_n)$. Since the backbone is decoder-only, we use the final token representation $h_{pool} = h_n$ as the query representation, which is passed through a lightweight classification head:
\begin{equation}
  h' = \text{Dropout} \Big( \text{GELU} ( W_1 \text{LayerNorm}(h_{pool}) + b_1 ) \Big), \quad \hat{y} = \text{Softmax} ( W_2 h' + b_2 ),
  \label{eq:head}
\end{equation}
where $W_1, W_2, b_1, b_2$ are learnable parameters. The resulting distribution $\hat{y} = \pi_\theta(y \mid x)$ represents the student router's routing prediction.

\subsection{Route-Only Distillation}
\label{sec:route-only}

Our first distillation strategy transfers only the teacher's routing decisions. The student router is trained using teacher-generated routing labels as supervision, with loss
\begin{equation}
  L_{route} = -\sum_{c \in \mathcal{Y}} y_c \log \hat{y}_c,
  \label{eq:lroute}
\end{equation}
where $y_c$ is the teacher routing label and $\hat{y}_c$ is the predicted routing probability. This objective transfers the teacher routing policy solely through routing labels and serves as the primary distillation baseline.

\subsection{Reasoning-Augmented Distillation}
\label{sec:reasoning-plus-route}

A routing label provides supervision only over the teacher's final routing decision. We therefore investigate whether routing-policy distillation benefits from additional supervision derived from teacher reasoning traces. In addition to the routing objective, the student router is trained to predict the teacher's reasoning sequence $r = (r_1, \ldots, r_T)$, with objective
\begin{equation}
  L_{reason} = -\sum_{t=1}^{T} \log P(r_t \mid r_{<t}, x).
  \label{eq:lreason}
\end{equation}
The final training objective combines both terms,
\begin{equation}
  L = \lambda_{route} L_{route} + \lambda_{reason} L_{reason}.
  \label{eq:ltotal}
\end{equation}
Because reasoning traces are used only during training, both distillation variants incur identical deployment cost. At inference time, the student router receives only the query and predicts a routing label through a single forward pass.




\section{Experimental Setup}
\label{sec:experimental-setup}

The goal of our experiments is to evaluate whether routing policies can be effectively compressed through routing-policy distillation and whether reasoning supervision improves policy transfer (\ref{sec:research-questions}).

\subsection{Research Questions}
\label{sec:research-questions}

We design our experiments to answer three questions.
\begin{description}
  \item[RQ1.] How effectively can a routing policy be transferred from an accurate but expensive teacher router to a compact student router?
  \item[RQ2.] Does augmenting routing supervision with teacher reasoning improve policy transfer relative to route-only distillation?
  \item[RQ3.] Do improvements in policy transfer translate into improvements in routing accuracy under independent oracle labels?
\end{description}
Answering RQ3 requires an evaluation target that does not simply re-measure performance against the teacher router. This is the role of the oracle labels (\ref{sec:oracle-routing-policy}). Oracle labels are used only for evaluation and provide an independent measure of routing accuracy; they are never used to supervise a router during training.

\subsection{Routing Label Spaces}
\label{sec:routing-label-spaces}

DistillRouter (\S\ref{sec:methodology}) is agnostic to the routing label space $\mathcal{Y}$. This section fixes the two routing settings evaluated in this paper as experimental configurations rather than methodological choices.

\paragraph{Binary Routing.} Our primary configuration frames routing as an escalation decision,
\begin{equation}
  \mathcal{Y}_{binary} = \{\texttt{small}, \texttt{large}\},
\end{equation}
where \texttt{small} denotes routing to whichever of $\{m_1, m_2\}$ is the cheapest candidate that answers the query correctly, and \texttt{large} denotes routing to $m_3$. Oracle labels (\ref{sec:oracle-routing-policy}) determine the correct assignment. This routing setting reflects the deployment scenario most relevant to cost-sensitive routing: whether a query can remain on a low-cost model or must be escalated to the highest-capability model.

\paragraph{Three-Way Routing.} To evaluate finer-grained routing decisions, we additionally consider a three-way routing label space that maps directly to the three candidate tiers in $\mathcal{M}$. This setting is evaluated as a generalization check rather than the primary experimental configuration.

\paragraph{Model-Scale Analysis.}
\label{sec:scaling-generalization}
Independently of routing setting, we repeat experiments using both 270M and 1B student-router backbones to assess whether routing-policy distillation behaves differently across model scales (\ref{sec:effect-of-scale}). This analysis is performed under both routing settings, completing the full routing-setting $\times$ backbone grid.

\subsection{Routing Environment}
\label{sec:datasets}

We fix all three before introducing either the teacher router or the student router.

\subsection{Teacher Configuration}
\label{sec:teacher-policy}
\label{sec:teacher_configuration}

The teacher router follows a two-stage prompting procedure. For each query, a zero-shot prompt first generates a teacher reasoning trace describing the query's reasoning requirements, and a few-shot prompt then predicts a routing label conditioned on both the query and the generated reasoning trace. Few-shot demonstrations are sampled from the calibration split (\ref{sec:datasets}) and remain fixed throughout training and evaluation. The teacher router is Qwen2.5-3B-Instruct (Table~\ref{tab:models}).

\subsection{Student Training}
\label{sec:training-protocol}
\label{sec:training_configuration}
\label{sec:distilled-routers}

We train the two routing-policy distillation variants introduced in Sections~\ref{sec:route-only} and~\ref{sec:reasoning-plus-route}: route-only distillation, where the student router learns exclusively from teacher routing labels, and reasoning-augmented distillation, where the student router additionally learns from teacher reasoning traces. All other aspects of training are identical, isolating the effect of reasoning supervision. Unless otherwise specified, all student routers are trained with the AdamW optimizer at a learning rate of $2 \times 10^{-5}$, batch size 8, bf16 mixed precision, and 3 training epochs. Models are selected using validation macro-F1 and evaluated once on the held-out test split. Macro-F1 is used because routing labels are imbalanced (Table~\ref{tab:splits}). Each main experiment is repeated across ten random seeds, with results reported as mean $\pm$ standard deviation.

\subsection{Evaluation Metrics}
\label{sec:evaluation_metrics}

We evaluate distilled routers along three dimensions: policy transfer, routing accuracy, and deployment efficiency.

\paragraph{Policy Transfer.}
\label{sec:routing-policy-transfer}
Policy transfer measures how closely a student router reproduces the teacher router's routing policy. We report accuracy and macro-F1 between student routing labels and teacher routing labels. This metric answers RQ1 and RQ2.

\paragraph{Routing Accuracy.}
\label{sec:routing-correctness}
Routing accuracy measures agreement with oracle labels rather than the teacher router. Accuracy and macro-F1 are computed against oracle labels and provide an independent measure of routing quality. This metric answers RQ3 by evaluating whether improvements in policy transfer correspond to improvements in routing accuracy.

\paragraph{Deployment Efficiency.}
\label{sec:efficiency}
We report routing latency and speedup relative to the teacher router. Latency is measured with batch size one on identical hardware for all methods. While the teacher router requires multiple prompted inference calls, a student router performs routing through a single forward pass. Deployment efficiency therefore quantifies the practical benefit of routing-policy distillation in real-world serving environments.
\section{Results}
\label{sec:results}

This paper's central claim is that routing-policy distillation enables efficient LLM routing: a compact, single-forward-pass student router can match or exceed the routing accuracy of an expensive multi-stage teacher router while operating at a fraction of the deployment cost. We first evaluate routing accuracy (RQ1, Section~\ref{sec:can-be-distilled}), then examine deployment efficiency (Section~\ref{sec:rq3-efficiency}), before analyzing the effect of reasoning supervision on policy transfer (RQ2, Section~\ref{sec:main-result}). We conclude with supporting analyses that investigate why distilled routers surpass teacher routing accuracy (Section~\ref{sec:distillation-improves-teacher}), the relationship between policy transfer and routing accuracy (Section~\ref{sec:teacher-fidelity-oracle}), scaling behavior (Section~\ref{sec:effect-of-scale}), and common routing errors (Section~\ref{sec:error-analysis}). Unless otherwise noted, all results use the primary configuration: binary routing with the 270M student-router backbone.

\paragraph{Comparison to RouteLLM.}
\label{sec:routellm-baseline}
RouteLLM \citep{ong2024routellm} trains a lightweight classifier to
predict, for a query, the probability that a strong model should be
used instead of a weak one, thresholded into a binary routing
decision --- methodologically the closest prior system to this paper's
binary label space. Of RouteLLM's released routers, only \texttt{bert}
runs without an external paid API: \texttt{mf} and \texttt{sw\_ranking}
call OpenAI's embedding API at inference, and \texttt{causal\_llm} ships
checkpoints tied to a specific 8B model, so we evaluate \texttt{bert}
(\texttt{routellm/bert\_gpt4\_augmented}) directly, unmodified, as
published. Critically, this checkpoint was trained on Chatbot Arena
human-preference battles between \texttt{gpt-4-1106-preview} and
\texttt{mixtral-8x7b-instruct-v0.1} --- a subjective response-quality
signal between two frontier-scale chat models, not the objective
task-correctness signal this paper's oracle labels provide, and not
calibrated to this paper's candidate pool at all. We therefore report
it as an out-of-the-box baseline, not a like-for-like retrained
comparison: we compute its win-rate score for every query, then
calibrate a single threshold on this paper's own train split to hit a
target escalation rate, at two targets --- matching route-only's own
56.1\% escalation rate (a cost-matched comparison) and matching the
oracle's true 65.1\% escalation rate (a generous, best-case
calibration for RouteLLM) --- before evaluating once on the test
split (no seed variance: the checkpoint and threshold are both fixed).

The results in Table~\ref{tab:mainperf} are mixed rather than a clean
win either way. RouteLLM's oracle agreement (0.571--0.588) is
\emph{lower} than even the teacher's (0.592), consistent with the
domain mismatch described above --- an off-the-shelf preference
classifier calibrated on GPT-4-vs-Mixtral quality differences has no
reason to transfer to deciding whether a 270M-parameter model can
solve a grade-school arithmetic problem. Yet its end-to-end accuracy
(0.404--0.421) is competitive with both distilled variants, edging out
reasoning-plus-route at the oracle-matched threshold. This is not a
contradiction: the binary deployment policy (\ref{sec:evaluation_metrics})
routes every \texttt{cheap} decision to the medium candidate regardless
of which specific queries are labeled \texttt{cheap}, so end-to-end
accuracy is more forgiving of routing-label disagreement than oracle
agreement is. Oracle agreement remains the more diagnostic metric for
routing quality specifically (\ref{sec:teacher-fidelity-oracle} makes
the general case for why), and on that metric RouteLLM's off-the-shelf
router underperforms every routing policy evaluated in this paper,
including the teacher it was never trained to compete with.

Although the downstream gains are smaller in magnitude than the
oracle-accuracy gains, they indicate that improved routing decisions
translate to practical benefits beyond routing metrics alone. Overall,

Across multiple configurations, the model variant with higher teacher
agreement does not necessarily achieve higher oracle agreement: at
three of the four configurations, the variant with higher teacher-F1
has \emph{lower} oracle-F1; at the fourth (binary/1B) the two rankings
agree, but the correctness gap is still not statistically reliable.
In particular, reasoning-augmented distillation improves fidelity at
the 1B scale but does not consistently improve correctness --- across
all four configurations, no configuration shows a statistically
reliable oracle-correctness advantage for either variant, even at the
two 1B configurations where the fidelity advantage is confirmed
(\ref{sec:main-result}).

To put this observation on statistical footing rather than relying on
the eight-point scatter alone, we compute the correlation between
teacher-agreement F1 and oracle F1. A naive Pearson correlation across
all eight configuration/variant points is strongly positive
($r = +0.97$, $p < 0.001$) --- but this is a structural confound, not
evidence that fidelity predicts correctness: binary-label-space
configurations score higher than 3-label configurations on \emph{both}
axes simply because binary is the easier classification problem
(Table~\ref{tab:oracle270m}), inflating the pooled correlation without
reflecting any relationship within a fixed configuration. Controlling
for label space --- a partial Pearson correlation over all 80
individual seed runs (10 seeds $\times$ 8 configuration/variant pairs),
residualizing both metrics against a binary-vs.-3-label indicator ---
removes this confound and eliminates the association: $r = -0.16$
($p = 0.17$, $n = 80$). The more direct test correlates the
reasoning-vs.-route-only \emph{change} in fidelity against the
corresponding change in oracle F1, one point per configuration
($n = 4$): Pearson $r = -0.45$ ($p = 0.55$); Spearman
$\rho = 0.00$ ($p = 1.0$) --- the ranking of which configurations gain
the most fidelity from reasoning supervision carries no information
about which configurations gain the most routing accuracy. All three
tests agree once the label-space confound is removed: the null
correlation visible in Figure~\ref{fig:fidelity-vs-correctness} is not
an artifact of a sparse scatter.

These results indicate that policy transfer and routing accuracy capture different aspects of routing behavior. Consequently,
policy transfer alone should not be used as a proxy for
routing accuracy; independent evaluation against oracle routing labels
remains necessary when assessing routing-policy transfer methods.

\subsection{Error Analysis}
\label{sec:error-analysis}

To better understand routing policy, we analyze confusion matrices
aggregated across all test predictions under the binary-routing
formulation, pooling every test-set prediction across all 10 seeds and
both datasets ($n = 10{,}000$ per variant) against oracle labels.

Against the oracle, the teacher itself under-escalates (large-tier
recall only 49.6\%, barely better than chance); both distilled
variants correct this specifically (68.6--70.7\% large-tier recall
vs.\ oracle), trading back some small-tier recall in exchange ---
explaining much of the routing-accuracy improvement observed throughout
Section~\ref{sec:can-be-distilled}. Apart from this correction, the two
distilled variants display highly similar error patterns (no cell
differs by more than $\sim$1.2 points). A smaller, related asymmetry
shows up against the teacher's own decision rather than the oracle: a
distilled model over-escalates (small$\to$large, 42--44\%) more than
it under-escalates (large$\to$small, 22--24\%) --- an ``err toward the
safer, more expensive tier'' bias that is separate from, and smaller
than, the oracle-referenced correction above. The remaining errors
largely reflect ambiguity near routing boundaries, where queries can
reasonably be assigned to either lower-cost or higher-capability
models.

These findings suggest that the primary benefit of distillation is not
broad improvement across all routing decisions, but rather correction
of a specific and important failure mode present in the teacher
router.
\section{Limitations}
\label{sec:limitations}

This study has several limitations. First, experiments are restricted to mathematical reasoning benchmarks, and the extent to which routing-policy distillation generalizes to other domains such as coding, instruction following, or open-domain question answering remains unverified. Second, DistillRouter is evaluated using a single teacher router and prompting strategy; alternative teacher policies may exhibit different transfer characteristics. Third, our scaling analysis includes only two student model sizes, limiting conclusions about how routing-policy distillation behaves across broader capacity ranges. Finally, reasoning is studied exclusively as a training-time supervision signal, and we do not investigate its interaction with inference-time reasoning-based routing methods. These limitations provide several directions for future work.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

We presented DistillRouter, a routing-policy distillation framework for efficient LLM routing. DistillRouter transfers routing policy from an expensive teacher router into a lightweight student model, enabling routing decisions through a single forward pass. Across multiple routing configurations, distilled routers consistently improved routing quality relative to the teacher while reducing routing latency by up to 150×. We further found that reasoning supervision improves policy transfer at larger scales but does not consistently improve routing correctness. Overall, our results demonstrate that routing policies can be effectively compressed through knowledge distillation, making lightweight routers a practical solution for large-scale LLM deployment.

\subsection{Epoch-Count Robustness}
\label{sec:epoch-count}

Every main-body result uses a 3-epoch budget
(\ref{sec:training-protocol}). We checked this at a single seed (42),
20 epochs, both label spaces.

Both label spaces peak well past epoch 3 (binary: route-only at epoch
2, reasoning-plus-route at epoch 6; 3-label: epoch 5 and 8
respectively), with validation macro-F1 gains of $0.08$--$0.12$ over
the 3-epoch checkpoint. On held-out test data, though, the gain is
smaller and inconsistent: route-only/binary --- this paper's primary
configuration --- is slightly \emph{worse} at its best 20-epoch
checkpoint (0.640) than the main protocol's 10-seed mean (0.651 $\pm$
0.005); the other three configurations show modest gains within
roughly one standard deviation.

Because route-only/binary is this paper's primary configuration, we
repeated it at 20 epochs across the same ten seeds used throughout the
main protocol, rather than relying on the single-seed check above.
The 20-epoch test macro-F1 is $0.653 \pm 0.013$, against the 3-epoch
main-protocol result of $0.651 \pm 0.005$ (Table~\ref{tab:main270m}).
A paired seed-by-seed bootstrap over the ten matched seeds gives a
mean improvement of $+0.002$ macro-F1 with a 95\% CI of
$[-0.006, +0.011]$, which includes zero: six of the ten seeds are
lower at 20 epochs than at 3 epochs, and seed-to-seed variance nearly
triples ($\pm 0.013$ vs.\ $\pm 0.005$). Extending training therefore
does not yield a reliable test-set improvement at this configuration,
despite the consistent validation-macro-F1 gains observed above; this
confirms the single-seed result was not an artifact of that one seed,
and supports retaining the 3-epoch, ten-seed protocol as this paper's
main result.
